Chess has a reputation for being complicated, but the rules themselves take about fifteen minutes to learn properly. The complexity comes later, from strategy — not from the rules. This guide covers everything you need to play a legal, confident game.

## The board and setup

Chess is played on an 8×8 board of alternating light and dark squares. Each player starts with 16 pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight pawns, arranged on the back two ranks. The rule of thumb for setup: "white on right" — the light-colored square is in each player's bottom-right corner — and the queen starts on her own color.

## How each piece moves

- **Pawn** — moves forward one square (two on its first move), captures diagonally one square forward, and cannot move backward.
- **Rook** — moves any number of squares horizontally or vertically.
- **Bishop** — moves any number of squares diagonally, staying on one color for the entire game.
- **Knight** — moves in an "L" shape: two squares in one direction, then one square perpendicular. It's the only piece that can jump over other pieces.
- **Queen** — combines the rook and bishop: any number of squares in any straight or diagonal direction.
- **King** — moves one square in any direction, but can never move into check.

## Check, checkmate, and stalemate

- **Check** means the king is under direct attack. The player in check must move out of it, block it, or capture the attacking piece — no other move is legal.
- **Checkmate** ends the game: the king is in check and there's no legal way to escape it.
- **Stalemate** is a draw: the player to move has no legal move at all, and their king is *not* in check. This trips up beginners constantly — a huge material advantage can still end in a stalemate draw if you're not careful near the endgame.

## The three special-case rules everyone forgets

### Castling
A king and rook can move together once per game, provided neither piece has moved yet, there are no pieces between them, the king is not in check, and the king doesn't pass through or land on a square that's under attack. The king moves two squares toward the rook, and the rook jumps to the square next to the king. This is the fastest way to get the king to safety.

### En passant
If a pawn moves two squares forward from its starting position and lands beside an opponent's pawn, that opponent's pawn can capture it *as if* it had only moved one square — but only immediately, on the very next move. Miss the window and the right disappears for that pawn.

### Pawn promotion
When a pawn reaches the opposite end of the board, it must promote to a queen, rook, bishop, or knight (not a king). Almost always players promote to a queen, but underpromotion to a knight is occasionally the stronger tactical choice.

## Draw rules beyond stalemate

- **Threefold repetition** — if the exact same position occurs three times (same player to move, same pieces, same rights), the player whose turn it is can claim a draw. The positions must match in who is to move, the pieces, and the available castling and en passant rights.
- **Fifty-move rule** — if each player has made 50 moves with no pawn move and no capture, the player to move can claim a draw. The game is drawn automatically at 75 moves each.
- **Insufficient material** — if neither side has enough pieces left to deliver checkmate (e.g. king vs. king, or king and bishop vs. king), the game is automatically drawn.
- **Mutual agreement** — players can agree to a draw once each has made at least one move (a tournament can restrict this).

## Common beginner rule mistakes

- Forgetting that the king can never move into check, even to capture.
- Trying to castle after the king or rook has already moved (not allowed, even if they later returned to their original squares).
- Missing en passant's one-move-only window.
- Assuming the game ends the moment a king is "trapped," rather than checking whether it's actually checkmate or just check.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can the king ever be captured?**
No. The game ends at checkmate — the position where capture would be unavoidable next move — before an actual king capture occurs.

**Is castling mandatory?**
No, it's optional, but it's almost always a good idea for king safety.

**What happens if I run out of time?**
In timed games, running out of the clock is a loss, provided your opponent has enough material to deliver checkmate; otherwise it's a draw.
