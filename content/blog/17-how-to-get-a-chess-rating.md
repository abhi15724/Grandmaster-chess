"Chess rating" actually means two very different things depending on what you're asking for. An **online rating** (chess.com, Lichess, or here) is instant and automatic — it starts the moment you play your first game. An **official FIDE rating** is a completely separate, much slower process that requires playing real, arbiter-run tournaments and registering with your national chess federation. Most people asking "how do I get a chess rating" actually want the first one — so here's both, starting with the easy one.

## Getting an online rating (the easy way)

This part requires nothing beyond playing:

1. **Pick a platform.** Chess.com, Lichess, and this site all generate a rating automatically — you don't request one or apply for it.
2. **Play a game.** Here, you get a starting rating of 1200 the moment you play your first game against the [AI](/play/ai) or [locally](/play/local) — no registration, sign-up, or fee required. It adjusts automatically after every game using the same Elo-style math [FIDE and every major platform use](/blog/chess-rating-elo-explained).
3. **Keep playing.** Your rating updates in real time and you can track it on your [dashboard](/dashboard) any time.

That's genuinely the entire process. The only real decision is which platform's rating pool you want to build a track record in — and nothing stops you from having a rating on several at once, since they're independent numbers.

## Getting an official FIDE rating (the real deal)

This is a different and considerably more involved process, since it requires supervised, arbiter-verified, over-the-board play. Based on FIDE's current rating regulations (effective March 2024):

1. **Register with your national chess federation and get a FIDE ID.** This is usually a short form with your name, birthdate, and country, sometimes with a small federation fee. Your national federation's website will have the process — in India, that's the All India Chess Federation (AICF).
2. **Find a FIDE-rated tournament.** Not every over-the-board tournament counts — it has to be officially registered with FIDE and run by a licensed arbiter. Your federation's website or a tournament-listing site will show which local events are FIDE-rated.
3. **Play at least 5 games against already-rated opponents**, within a 26-month window (they don't all have to be in one tournament).
4. **Score at least half a point** — one win or one draw — against those rated opponents. Games where an opponent defaults without making a move don't count toward this.
5. **Reach a rating performance of at least 1400.** This is the part that trips people up: under FIDE's current regulations, your first rating only gets *published* if your performance across those games works out to 1400 or higher. Play the required games but perform below that, and you stay formally unrated until a stronger result crosses the threshold — the games still count toward your eligibility, they just don't produce a public number yet.
6. **Wait for the tournament organizer to submit results to FIDE.** This isn't automatic on your end — the organizing arbiter submits an official results file, typically within 10–30 days of the event.
7. **Check the published list.** FIDE updates its official rating list on the first of every month. Once you're on it, you can look yourself up at ratings.fide.com using your FIDE ID.

## Quick comparison

| | Online rating | Official FIDE rating |
|---|---|---|
| How fast | Instant, from your first game | Weeks to months, tied to monthly FIDE list publication |
| Cost | Free | Often a small federation registration fee |
| Where you play | Any device, anywhere | In-person, arbiter-run tournaments only |
| What it's used for | Matchmaking, personal tracking, bragging rights | Official rankings, title norms (FM/IM/GM), tournament seeding |
| Starting point | Fixed default (1200 here) | Calculated from your actual performance — no fixed starting number |

Neither one is "more real" than the other — they just measure different things. An online rating is a great, frictionless way to track your own improvement and find well-matched opponents. A FIDE rating is what you need if you're chasing official titles, national rankings, or eligibility for FIDE-rated events later on.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a FIDE rating to play chess online?**
No. Online ratings and FIDE ratings are completely independent systems — you can play thousands of rated online games without ever touching the FIDE system.

**What's the lowest possible FIDE rating?**
Under the current 2024 regulations, FIDE only publishes a first rating once it's calculated at 1400 or above — there's no published rating below that threshold, even though your qualifying games still count toward eligibility.

**Can I lose my FIDE rating if I stop playing?**
Your published rating stays on record, but you may be marked "inactive" after a long period without rated games. It doesn't reset to zero or disappear.

**Is my online chess rating the same number as a FIDE rating would be?**
No — different platforms use different player pools and K-factors, and none of them are directly equivalent to FIDE's system. A 1600 here, on Chess.com, on Lichess, and in FIDE's system are four different numbers that don't convert cleanly into each other.
