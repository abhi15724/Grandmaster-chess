If you've only ever played chess online, there's an official rule you've likely never encountered: touch it, move it. In sanctioned over-the-board chess, picking up a piece isn't a casual "let me consider this" gesture — it's a binding commitment, enforced by an arbiter if needed.

## The rule itself

Under FIDE's official rules, if a player **deliberately touches** one of their own pieces during their turn, they must move that piece, provided it has at least one legal move available. If a player touches one of the **opponent's** pieces, they must capture it, provided a legal capture is possible.

That's the entire rule, but it has real teeth in serious play: an arbiter can enforce it even if the player changes their mind mid-thought, and it's taken seriously enough that scholastic and tournament players are explicitly taught to think before they reach for a piece, not while their hand is already on it.

## The escape hatch: "I adjust" (j'adoube)

There's one legitimate exception, and it has to happen in a specific order: if a piece is sitting slightly crooked on its square and a player wants to straighten it without that counting as a move, they must say **"I adjust"** (traditionally the French **"j'adoube"**) **before** touching the piece — not after. Announce it first, then adjust the piece, and it doesn't trigger the touch-move obligation. Touch first and explain after, and the rule still applies.

## What happens if the touched piece has no legal moves

If a player touches a piece that genuinely has no legal move available (for example, a pinned piece that would expose the king to check if moved), there's no penalty — they're free to move any other piece instead. The rule only binds you to a piece that actually *can* legally move.

## Why online chess doesn't enforce this

This is the part that surprises players moving between formats: essentially no online platform enforces touch-move, including here. Digital interfaces let you pick up, drag, hover, and reconsider a piece before releasing it — there's no equivalent to physically lifting a wooden piece off a board in front of an arbiter. That's a genuinely different experience from tournament play, not a lesser one — it's just built around a different interaction model (clicking and dragging) that doesn't map onto a rule designed for physical pieces and human judgment calls.

## Why it matters if you're moving from online to over-the-board play

Players who learn primarily online sometimes get a real surprise at their first in-person tournament: reaching for a piece to "just look at the position from here" and then being told, correctly, that they now have to move it. If you're planning to play in a real tournament, it's worth building the habit of calculating fully before your hand leaves your lap — treat every touch as final, the same way an arbiter will.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the touch-move rule enforced in online chess?**
No, essentially no online platform enforces it, including here — you can pick up, drag, and reconsider a piece before committing to a move, unlike in official over-the-board play.

**What do I say if I need to fix a crooked piece without moving it?**
Say "I adjust" (or the traditional French "j'adoube") before you touch the piece, not after — announcing it first is what exempts the adjustment from the touch-move rule.

**What if I touch a piece that has no legal moves?**
There's no penalty — if the touched piece genuinely can't legally move (for example, it's pinned), you're free to move a different piece instead.

**Does touching an opponent's piece mean I have to capture it?**
Yes, if a legal capture with that piece is available — touching an opponent's piece obligates you to capture it, the same way touching your own piece obligates you to move it.

Since online play doesn't enforce touch-move, you're free to explore the board freely against the [AI](/play/ai) — just know the habit is different if you ever sit down for an over-the-board tournament.
