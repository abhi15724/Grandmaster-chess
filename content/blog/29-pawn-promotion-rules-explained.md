A pawn that survives the trip to the far side of the board doesn't just sit there proudly — it has to transform, immediately, as part of the same move that got it there. Here's exactly how that works, and the one situation where the "obvious" choice isn't actually the best one.

## The basic rule

The moment a pawn reaches the **final rank** — the 8th rank for White, the 1st rank for Black — it **must** promote, right then, as part of that same move. It cannot simply stay a pawn and sit on that square. The player chooses which piece it becomes: **queen, rook, bishop, or knight**. It cannot become a king, and it cannot remain a pawn.

## Yes, you can have more than one queen

This trips up a lot of newer players: there's no rule limiting you to a single queen. If a second pawn reaches the final rank later in the same game, it can promote to a second queen — chess doesn't restrict promotion based on what's already on the board. In digital play this is obviously a non-issue; in physical over-the-board play with a standard set, players traditionally use an upside-down rook to represent a second queen if a spare one isn't available, though most tournament sets now include extra queens specifically for this.

## Underpromotion: when a queen isn't actually the best choice

Choosing a rook, bishop, or knight instead of a queen is called **underpromotion**, and while it's rare, it's not just a curiosity — there are real, concrete reasons a stronger piece isn't always the right one:

- **Avoiding stalemate.** If promoting to a queen would leave the opponent with no legal moves while not in check, that's an immediate draw by stalemate — even in a position that was completely winning beforehand. Promoting to a rook or bishop instead can sometimes keep the position winning by not accidentally trapping the opponent's king with no moves.
- **Delivering immediate checkmate with a knight.** A knight's unique movement pattern can occasionally deliver a checkmate on the spot that a queen's more powerful, but differently-shaped, movement can't — because the queen would either not attack the right square or would allow an escape a knight's L-shaped move cuts off.
- **Avoiding a check that hands the opponent a tempo.** In rare cases, promoting to a queen would allow the opponent an immediate favorable response (like a check of their own) that promoting to a quieter piece avoids.

None of these come up in the overwhelming majority of games — most promotions really are just "promote to queen, obviously" — but underpromotion puzzles are a genuine, recurring category in chess tactics training precisely because the "obvious" choice is occasionally a real mistake.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you have two queens in chess?**
Yes — there's no limit on how many pieces of a given type you can have through promotion. If a second pawn reaches the final rank, it can also promote to a queen.

**What can a pawn promote to?**
Queen, rook, bishop, or knight — the player's choice. It cannot stay a pawn and cannot become a king.

**Why would anyone promote to something other than a queen?**
Most often to avoid an immediate stalemate that a queen promotion would cause, or because a knight's specific movement pattern can deliver checkmate in a way a queen's movement can't in that exact position.

**Does promotion happen automatically, or do I have to choose?**
You choose — every legitimate chess interface, including here, will prompt you to pick which piece your pawn promotes to the moment it reaches the final rank.
