Castling is the only move in chess where two pieces move at once — and it comes with five separate conditions that all have to be true simultaneously. Miss one, and it's not a legal move, even if the board looks fine at a glance.

## What castling actually does

Castling moves your king two squares toward one of your rooks, and that rook jumps to the square the king just passed over — all in a single move. There are two versions:

- **Kingside castling (O-O)** — the king moves toward the rook on the h-file (the "short" side), ending up on g1 (or g8 for Black), with the rook landing on f1 (or f8).
- **Queenside castling (O-O-O)** — the king moves toward the rook on the a-file (the "long" side), ending up on c1 (or c8), with the rook landing on d1 (or d8).

## The five conditions, all required

Every one of these has to be true, or castling isn't legal:

1. **Neither the king nor that specific rook has moved yet** — this is per-piece, not per-side: if your kingside rook has moved but your queenside rook hasn't, you can still castle queenside as long as the king also hasn't moved.
2. **No pieces between the king and that rook.** Every square in between must be empty.
3. **The king is not currently in check.** You cannot castle your way out of check.
4. **The king does not pass through a square that's under attack.** Even if the king isn't in check right now, it can't castle through an attacked square on its way.
5. **The king does not land on a square that's under attack.** The destination square has to be safe too.

## The two mistakes almost everyone makes at some point

**Mistake 1: thinking a rook under attack blocks castling.** It doesn't. Only the king's safety matters for this rule — the rook can be attacked, can even be captured the very next move, and castling is still completely legal. Players very commonly hesitate here for no reason.

**Mistake 2: assuming castling rights come back if the piece returns to its original square.** They don't. The moment your king or a rook moves — even one square, even if you immediately move it right back — castling rights for that side are gone permanently for the rest of the game. There's no way to "undo" the loss.

## Why castling exists

Castling does two useful things in one move: it tucks the king away from the center of the board (where most of the early tactical fighting happens) and it activates a rook that would otherwise sit passively in the corner for many moves. That's exactly why "castle early" is one of the [core opening principles](/blog/best-chess-openings-for-beginners) taught to every beginner — a king still in the center on move 10 is usually a sign something's gone wrong with development, not a deliberate strategy.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you castle if your rook is under attack?**
Yes. Only the king's current square, the squares it passes through, and its destination square need to be safe — the rook's safety doesn't factor into whether castling is legal.

**Can you castle out of check?**
No. If your king is currently in check, castling is not a legal move under any circumstances, even if the resulting position would otherwise satisfy every other condition.

**Do castling rights come back if I move my rook and then move it back?**
No. Once the king or a specific rook has moved, castling rights involving that piece are lost for the rest of the game, permanently — moving it back to its original square doesn't restore them.

**Can you castle queenside and kingside in the same game?**
No — you can only castle once per game, and only to one side. After castling, the move is complete and can't be repeated or switched to the other side later.
