Chess didn't start as chess. It started as a 6th-century Indian war game called chaturanga, and it took roughly 1,400 years, three continents, and a complete rewrite of its most powerful piece before it became the game played today.

## It started in India, as a four-part army

Chaturanga emerged in India around the 6th century CE — the name is Sanskrit for "four divisions," referring to the four arms of an ancient Indian army the pieces represented: infantry, cavalry, elephants, and chariots. Those four pieces are the direct ancestors of the pawn, knight, bishop, and rook. It was already played on an 8×8 board, and already involved the idea of threatening an opposing king — but the rules, piece movements, and win conditions were considerably simpler than modern chess, and historians still debate exactly how it evolved from earlier board games.

## Persia gave it its name and its philosophy

By around 600 CE, chaturanga had reached Sassanid Persia and become "chatrang," later "shatranj." This is where a lot of chess's vocabulary actually comes from: the Persian word for king, *shah*, is where "check" comes from, and the phrase *shah mat* — "the king is helpless" — is the direct ancestor of "checkmate." The Persians treated the game as more than entertainment; it became associated with wisdom and nobility, referenced by poets and philosophers, and played at royal courts.

## The Islamic world turned it into a serious intellectual pursuit

Following the Arab conquest of Persia in the 7th century, shatranj spread rapidly across the Islamic world, reaching North Africa and eventually Spain and Sicily by around the 8th–10th centuries. This period mattered enormously for chess's development: scholars like al-Adli and as-Suli wrote some of the earliest known chess manuals and strategic treatises during the Islamic Golden Age, and the oldest fully recorded chess game in existence — found in a 10th-century manuscript — was played between the Baghdad historian al-Suli and his student. Chess theory, as a written, studied discipline rather than just a pastime, effectively begins here.

## Europe rewrote the rules — and the queen got a promotion

Chess reached Europe through Islamic Spain (Al-Andalus) and Sicily, and separately through the Byzantine Empire into Eastern Europe. For centuries it was played there roughly the same way it had been in the Islamic world — including a painfully weak queen piece (then called the "counselor" or *firzan*) that could only move one square diagonally.

That changed dramatically in the late 15th century, concentrated in Spain and Italy. The queen was rebuilt into the long-range, all-directions piece it is today, the bishop gained its modern diagonal range, and rules like castling, the pawn's two-square first move, and pawn promotion were introduced. The transformation was so dramatic that the new version was sometimes nicknamed "Queen's Chess" or "Mad Queen Chess" at the time. Some historians have connected the queen's sudden rise in power to the era's powerful reigning European queens, most notably Isabella I of Castile — an appealing theory, though it's more historical speculation than settled fact. What's not in dispute is the outcome: by the end of the 15th century, chess was recognizably the same game played today, and the first printed chess book appeared around 1495.

## Codification: tournaments, titles, and a governing body

Chess spent the next few centuries as a popular but informally organized pastime before the 19th century turned it into an organized competitive sport. London hosted the first international chess tournament in 1851, won by Adolf Anderssen — the same year Anderssen played the legendary "Immortal Game" against Lionel Kieseritzky, still studied today for its audacious sacrifices. In 1886, Wilhelm Steinitz became the first officially recognized World Chess Champion, beating Johannes Zukertort in a match spanning three cities. In 1924, the Fédération Internationale des Échecs (FIDE) was founded in Paris to govern international chess, standardize its rules, and organize the World Championship cycle that continues today.

## The computer era

Chess became a serious testbed for artificial intelligence research through the 20th century, culminating in a genuinely historic moment: in 1997, IBM's Deep Blue defeated reigning World Champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match — the first time a computer had beaten a reigning World Champion under standard tournament conditions. It reshaped how seriously the world took machine intelligence, and it kicked off the modern era of chess engines that [now function as both opponents and study tools](/blog/how-chess-ai-engines-work) for players at every level.

## Chess goes online

The final major shift is the most recent: chess moved from boards and clocks to browsers and apps. Platforms built around online play grew steadily through the 2000s and 2010s, then saw a massive surge during the COVID-19 pandemic, when millions of newly housebound people picked up the game — a surge amplified further by Netflix's *The Queen's Gambit* in 2020, which briefly made chess sets one of the best-selling items in the world. Chess today is arguably more widely played than at any point in its 1,400-year history, and — fittingly, given where this whole story started — India is currently home to one of the [fastest-growing and highest-achieving chess scenes on Earth](/blog/india-highest-rated-chess-player).

## Frequently asked questions

**Where did chess originate?**
Chess originated in India around the 6th century CE as a game called chaturanga, which spread through Persia and the Islamic world before reaching Europe and evolving into the modern game.

**Why is it called "checkmate"?**
"Checkmate" comes from the Persian phrase *shah mat*, meaning "the king is helpless" — a term that traveled essentially unchanged through Arabic and into European languages as chess spread west.

**When did chess get its modern rules?**
The biggest single change — the queen and bishop gaining their modern long-range movement — happened in Spain and Italy in the late 15th century. Most of the rest of chess's modern rule set was in place by then, though some refinements (like the 50-move rule and formal draw regulations) came later as the game became more formally organized.

**Who was the first official World Chess Champion?**
Wilhelm Steinitz, who claimed the title in 1886 after defeating Johannes Zukertort.
