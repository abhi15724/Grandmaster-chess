Honest answer up front: if you want the single most complete free chess platform, full stop, it's **Lichess** — 100% free, open-source, funded by donations, with no paywall on anything. That's not a hedge, and you don't need to keep reading to get the real answer. What's actually useful is understanding *why*, and where a different platform — including this one — might genuinely fit what you're looking for better.

## Lichess: the most complete free option

Lichess launched in 2010, is run as a nonprofit, and funds itself entirely through donations. The result is unusual in software: there's no premium tier at all. Every tool is available to every user, free, from day one:

- **Unlimited Stockfish analysis** on any game or position, with no daily cap
- **An opening explorer** covering both a 10-million-game master database and a 4-billion-game online database, so you can see what moves strong players actually make from any position
- **Over 4 million puzzles**, filterable by tactical theme (forks, pins, skewers, and more)
- **Endgame tablebases** for positions with 7 or fewer pieces
- **Zero ads, zero upsells**, on a fast, functional (if not flashy) interface

The one thing worth knowing before you compare ratings across platforms: Lichess uses a different rating system and starting point (Glicko-2, starting at 1500) than Chess.com (Glicko-1, starting at 1200), so a 1700 on Lichess and a 1700 on Chess.com are not the same playing strength — typically a 200–300 point gap for equivalent skill. Neither number is "wrong," they're just [not directly comparable](/blog/chess-rating-elo-explained).

## Chess.com: the biggest, most polished option

Chess.com launched in 2007 and has grown into the largest chess platform by far — over 150 million registered accounts, the biggest active opponent pool, celebrity streamers, and regular events featuring the world's top players. Its free tier gives you unlimited games across every time control, but meters the tools that actually help you *improve*: analysis depth, the lesson library, and game archive access are capped, with the paid tiers (Gold, Platinum, Diamond — Diamond runs roughly $150/year) unlocking the rest.

That's not a criticism — it's a genuinely good product, and plenty of players happily pay for the structured lessons and full analysis. It's just a different model than Lichess's "everything free, always." If your priority is the largest possible pool of opponents and the most polished mobile app experience, Chess.com is the honest pick, paid tier or not.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best completely free chess platform?**
Lichess is the most complete free option — every feature (analysis, puzzles, opening explorer, tablebases) is free with no premium tier, funded entirely by donations rather than subscriptions or ads.

**Is Chess.com actually free?**
Yes, Chess.com's free tier includes unlimited games across all time controls, but caps analysis depth, lesson access, and game archives — the paid tiers exist specifically to remove those caps.

**Why is my rating different on different chess platforms?**
Each platform uses its own rating system and starting point, so the same skill level can show a noticeably different number — a 200–300 point gap between Lichess and Chess.com for similar strength is normal, not a sign either system is "wrong."

**Do I need an account to play chess online for free?**
On Lichess, no — you can play anonymously. On Chess.com, generally yes. Here, no account is needed either — you can start a game against the AI or a local match immediately.
